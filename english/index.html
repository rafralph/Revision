1

Descriptive or narrative writing

English Language · Paper 1 Q5 · 40 marks

The picture ideaA great description is a camera that moves slowly. Zoom in on one place, then shift time, weather or mood, and let small details carry the feeling.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 1 Question 5 is worth 40 marks: 24 for content and organisation, 16 for technical accuracy. It is the biggest question in English Language. Spend 45 minutes on it: 5 minutes to plan, 35 to write, 5 to check.

You choose one task. Usually it is a description based on a picture, or a story with a given title or idea.

To plan a description, use five "camera shots":

  1. Wide shot: the whole place, the weather and the light.
  2. Zoom in on one small detail.
  3. Bring in a person or a movement.
  4. Shift: change the time, the weather or the mood.
  5. End by returning to the first image, but changed (a circular ending).

For a story, use only one or two characters and one main event. Start close to the action. Do not try to tell a whole life.

To get the top marks:

  • Vary your sentence openings (an adverb, a verb, a prepositional phrase).
  • Use a one-word or very short sentence for impact.
  • Choose precise verbs and nouns, not many adjectives.
  • Use paragraphs of different lengths on purpose.

CAUTIONDo not write a list of five senses. Choose details that build one mood.

Example exam question

Write a description of a busy market as it closes for the night, as suggested by a picture. (24 marks for content and organisation, 16 for technical accuracy)

[40 marks]
  1. Plan five shots: market at dusk → a single stall → the trader packing up → rain and darkness arrive → the empty square
  2. Open with a short, striking sentence, not 'One day…'
  3. Use precise verbs and a mix of sentence lengths and openings
  4. End with a circular image that shows the change in mood
  5. Leave 5 minutes to check spelling, full stops and capital letters

Answer: Model opening (invented): Dusk. The market sagged like a tired animal. Under a single swinging bulb, the fishmonger scraped silver scales into a bucket, and the last light slid off the cobbles and into the drains.

Writing to present a viewpoint

English Language · Paper 2 Q5 · 40 marks

The picture ideaA persuasive piece is a staircase. Each paragraph is one step: a point, evidence for it, and a reason the reader should care.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 2 Question 5 is worth 40 marks: 24 for content and organisation, 16 for technical accuracy. You get a statement and a form, such as an article, a letter or a speech.

Recent statements were about holidays close to home, public transport, fast fashion, and the risks of travel. Forms were articles and a speech.

Plan in this order:

  1. Decide your view. You can agree, disagree, or take a balanced view, but be clear.
  2. Choose 3 or 4 main points. Put the strongest last.
  3. Add one counter-argument and answer it.
  4. Plan an opening hook and a final call to action.

Match the form: a letter needs "Dear…" and a sign-off; an article needs a headline; a speech speaks directly to the audience ("you", "we").

Useful methods: direct address, rhetorical questions, facts and statistics (you can invent realistic ones), anecdote, a list of three, and emotive language. Use them with purpose, not as a checklist.

CAUTIONDo not write a list of DAFOREST techniques. Examiners reward a clear, controlled argument.

Example exam question

'Holidays don't need to be faraway and expensive. They just need to give people a break.' Write an article for a magazine in which you argue your point of view on this statement.

[40 marks]
  1. Headline and hook: a short anecdote about a rainy day trip that was still the best holiday
  2. Point 1: rest comes from a change of routine, not distance
  3. Counter-argument: 'travel broadens the mind' — answer it: so can a new town an hour away
  4. Point 2 (strongest): cost and climate — cheaper, greener breaks are open to everyone
  5. Call to action: 'This summer, look closer to home.'

Answer: Model hook (invented): Last August, my family's 'holiday' was a tent, a field forty minutes from home and six days of rain. It was the best week of my year. So why do we believe that a break only counts if it needs a passport?

Evaluating a statement about a text

English Language · Paper 1 Q4 · 20 marks

The picture ideaQ4 is a debate with a student. You are the judge: decide how far you agree, then prove it with evidence and the writer's methods.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 1 Question 4 is worth 20 marks. A student makes a statement about the second half of the source. You evaluate how far you agree.

Use the "View – Evidence – Method – Effect" pattern in each paragraph:

  1. View: say how far you agree with one part of the statement.
  2. Evidence: a short quotation.
  3. Method: name what the writer does (word choice, image, sentence form, structure).
  4. Effect: explain what it makes the reader think or feel. Link back to the statement.

Write 3 or 4 paragraphs in 20 minutes. Respond to both parts of the statement. You can partly agree.

CAUTIONOnly use the lines the question gives. Quotations from outside them get no marks.

Example exam question

Invented extract: The kitchen was a furnace. Steam crawled up the windows and hung there, heavy and grey, while pans hissed like angry cats. Mrs Hale moved through it all without a sound, her sleeves rolled, her face set like stone. Only when the boy dropped the plate did she stop. She knelt, picked up the pieces one by one, and said nothing at all.

A student said: 'In this part of the story, the writer makes Mrs Hale seem cold, but the reader can tell she cares.' To what extent do you agree?

[20 marks]
  1. View: I agree that she first seems cold
  2. Evidence and method: the simile 'her face set like stone' suggests she is hard and shows no feeling
  3. View: but I agree more with the second part
  4. Evidence and method: she 'knelt' and picked up the pieces 'one by one' — slow, gentle verbs show patience, not anger
  5. Effect: her silence protects the boy; the writer lets actions, not words, show care

Answer: Mostly agree: the stone simile makes her seem cold, but her slow, gentle actions show quiet care.

Comparing writers' perspectives

English Language · Paper 2 Q4 · 16 marks

The picture ideaQ4 is two people talking about the same thing. Find what each one feels, then show how each makes you feel it.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 2 Question 4 is worth 16 marks. You compare how two writers convey their different feelings and perspectives on the same topic. One source is usually from the 19th century, and one is modern.

Write 3 comparative paragraphs. In each paragraph:

  1. State the difference (or similarity) in their views.
  2. Writer A: quotation, method, effect.
  3. Use a comparative connective: "whereas", "in contrast", "similarly".
  4. Writer B: quotation, method, effect.

Talk about perspective: why might each writer feel this way? Think about their time, their purpose and their situation.

CAUTIONCompare in every paragraph. Two separate essays score low.

Example exam question

Invented Source A (1850s letter): The engine shrieked like a thing alive, and we were flung into the dark at a speed no horse could dream of. I confess I gripped the seat and prayed.
Invented Source B (modern blog): Another Monday, another 7.42. I press my forehead to the window and count the stations like sheep.

Compare how the writers convey their different feelings about train travel.

[16 marks]
  1. Difference: Writer A finds the train frightening and amazing; Writer B finds it dull
  2. A: the simile 'like a thing alive' makes the engine a monster; 'flung' shows lack of control
  3. Whereas B: 'Another Monday, another 7.42' — repetition shows routine and boredom
  4. Perspective: in the 1850s train speed was new and shocking; today it is ordinary

Answer: A is thrilled and scared (monster simile, 'flung'); whereas B is bored (repetition, counting 'like sheep'), because trains are now routine.

Language analysis in non-fiction

English Language · Paper 2 Q3 · 12 marks

The picture ideaZoom in on a single word. Ask what it suggests, what it makes you picture, and why the writer chose it over a plainer word.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 2 Question 3 is worth 12 marks. It asks how the writer uses language in one section of Source A or B. Spend 12–15 minutes.

Use the "What – How – Why" method:

  1. What: the idea the writer gives (for example, the storm is violent).
  2. How: a short quotation and the method — word class, simile, metaphor, personification, sentence form, tone.
  3. Why: the effect on the reader. Zoom in on one word and explore its connotations.

Higher-level answers explore more than one meaning, and link several methods together.

CAUTION"It makes the reader want to read on" is too general. Say exactly what the reader imagines or feels.

Example exam question

Invented extract: The storm arrived without manners. It slammed the shutters, clawed at the roof tiles and hurled the rain sideways, as if the sky itself were furious with us.

How does the writer use language to describe the storm?

[12 marks]
  1. What: the storm is violent and deliberate, like an angry person
  2. How: personification — 'without manners', 'slammed', 'clawed'
  3. Why: 'clawed' suggests an animal attacking, so the house seems under siege
  4. Zoom: 'furious' makes the sky a judge punishing them, so the reader feels small and powerless

Answer: Personification ('slammed', 'clawed', 'furious') turns the storm into a violent attacker, so the reader feels the family is helpless.

Language analysis in fiction

English Language · Paper 1 Q2 · 8 marks

The picture ideaSame tools as Paper 2 Q3, in a smaller box: about two developed points in 10 minutes.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 1 Question 2 is worth 8 marks. It asks how the writer uses language to describe something in a short section of the fiction source. Spend about 10 minutes.

Make 2 or 3 developed points. In each one:

  1. Give a short quotation.
  2. Name the method if you can (simile, verb, adjective, sentence form). The name alone gets no marks.
  3. Explain the effect, and zoom in on one word.

You can use subject terminology such as noun, verb, adverb, metaphor and simile. Use it accurately.

CAUTIONDo not write about structure here. That is Question 3.

Example exam question

Invented extract: The kitchen was a furnace. Steam crawled up the windows and hung there, heavy and grey, while pans hissed like angry cats.

How does the writer use language to describe the kitchen?

[8 marks]
  1. Metaphor 'The kitchen was a furnace' — not just hot but unbearable, almost dangerous
  2. Verb 'crawled' personifies the steam as a slow creature; it feels oppressive
  3. Simile 'hissed like angry cats' makes the pans aggressive; the kitchen feels hostile

Answer: Metaphor, personified verb and animal simile make the kitchen hot, oppressive and hostile.

Structure: how the text is built

English Language · Paper 1 Q3 · 8 marks

The picture ideaStructure is where the camera points and when it moves. Track the focus from the beginning, through the middle, to the end.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 1 Question 3 is worth 8 marks. It asks how the writer structures the whole source to interest the reader.

Structure is not about single words. It is about order and focus:

  • What does the writer focus on at the start? Why?
  • How and where does the focus shift (from place to person, outside to inside, present to past)?
  • What happens at the end? Is it a cliffhanger, a circle, or a change in mood?

Write 3 paragraphs: beginning, middle (shift), end. Use terms such as opening, shift in focus, flashback, dialogue, one-line paragraph, foreshadowing and cyclical structure.

CAUTIONAlways say why the writer does it: to create mystery, build tension or make us sympathise.

Example exam question

An invented story opens with a description of a stormy harbour, then focuses on one old fisherman, then flashes back to his youth, and ends with a single line of dialogue: 'He's not coming back.' How has the writer structured the text to interest the reader?

[8 marks]
  1. Beginning: a wide view of the stormy harbour sets a tense, threatening atmosphere
  2. Shift: the focus narrows to one fisherman, so we care about a person, not just a place
  3. Middle: the flashback to his youth creates sympathy and hints at a lost son
  4. End: the one-line dialogue is a shock and leaves an unanswered question

Answer: Wide setting → narrow focus → flashback → shock line: builds tension, sympathy and mystery.

Summary of differences

English Language · Paper 2 Q2 · 8 marks

The picture ideaA summary is a spot-the-difference game. Find a fact in each source, then explain what each one suggests.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 2 Question 2 is worth 8 marks. You write a summary of the differences (or similarities) between two things in Source A and Source B.

This question tests understanding, not language analysis. Do not analyse methods.

Write 2 or 3 paragraphs:

  1. Make a clear point about Source A, with a short quotation.
  2. Infer: say what it suggests ("This implies…").
  3. Use "whereas" and do the same for Source B.

The top marks are for perceptive inferences: reading between the lines.

CAUTIONDo not write about language techniques here. That gets no marks in Q2.

Example exam question

Using the two invented train sources in the 'Comparing perspectives' guide, write a summary of the differences between the journeys.

[8 marks]
  1. A: the train goes 'at a speed no horse could dream of' — this implies rail travel was new and astonishing
  2. Whereas B: 'Another Monday, another 7.42' suggests the journey is a dull daily routine
  3. A is frightening ('gripped the seat'), whereas B is so calm the writer 'counts the stations like sheep', implying boredom

Answer: A: new, fast and frightening; B: routine and boring — with inferences for each.

Retrieval and true statements

English Language · Paper 1 Q1 + Paper 2 Q1 · 8 marks

The picture ideaThese are the easiest 8 marks in the exam. Read only the lines given, and copy facts exactly.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Paper 1 Question 1 (4 marks) asks you to list four things from a few lines. Paper 2 Question 1 (4 marks) asks you to shade four true statements from a list of eight.

  1. Draw a box around the lines in the question.
  2. Only use those lines.
  3. For P1 Q1, write short facts. You can quote or paraphrase.
  4. For P2 Q1, shade exactly four boxes. If you shade more than four, you lose marks.

Spend only about 5 minutes on each. Save time for the big questions.

CAUTIONDo not infer. Q1 wants facts that are stated.

Example exam question

Invented extract: The kitchen was a furnace. Steam crawled up the windows and hung there, heavy and grey, while pans hissed like angry cats. Mrs Hale moved through it all without a sound, her sleeves rolled.

List four things about the kitchen or Mrs Hale from this extract.

[4 marks]
  1. The kitchen is very hot
  2. There is steam on the windows
  3. The pans are hissing
  4. Mrs Hale has her sleeves rolled up

Answer: Four stated facts, e.g. hot kitchen; steam on windows; pans hissing; sleeves rolled.

Technical accuracy (spelling, punctuation, grammar)

English Language · Q5 on both papers · 32 marks

The picture ideaTechnical accuracy is 32 marks you can practise like a sport: a few drills, done every time, until they are automatic.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

In each Question 5, 16 of the 40 marks are for technical accuracy. That is 32 marks across English Language — 20% of the whole GCSE.

Check these every time:

  1. Full stops, not commas, between sentences (no comma splices).
  2. Capital letters for names, places and "I".
  3. Apostrophes: it's = it is; its = belonging to it.
  4. Homophones: there / their / they're; your / you're; to / too.
  5. A range of punctuation, used correctly: colon, semi-colon, dash, question mark.
  6. A range of sentence types: simple, compound and complex.

Leave 5 minutes at the end to proofread. Read each sentence slowly, as if aloud.

CAUTIONA semi-colon joins two full sentences that are closely linked. Do not use it before a list of words.

Example exam question

Correct the errors: its late, the market is closing, there packing away there stalls and i can hear the rain

[4 marks]
  1. 'its' → 'It's' (it is) and capital at the start
  2. Comma splice: 'late. The market is closing.'
  3. 'there packing away there stalls' → 'They're packing away their stalls'
  4. 'i' → 'I', and a full stop at the end

Answer: It's late. The market is closing. They're packing away their stalls, and I can hear the rain.

1

Shakespeare essay (e.g. Macbeth)

English Literature · Lit Paper 1 Section A · 34 marks

The picture ideaStart with the extract, then zoom out to the whole play. The examiner wants a journey: how the character or theme changes.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Literature Paper 1 Section A is worth 34 marks (30 plus 4 for spelling, punctuation and grammar). You get an extract and a question about a character or theme. You must write about the extract and the whole play.

The question can be about a character, such as Macbeth or Lady Macbeth, or a theme, such as ambition, guilt or the supernatural.

Plan 4 paragraphs in 50 minutes:

  1. The extract: two or three close points on language.
  2. Earlier in the play.
  3. Later in the play: how it changes.
  4. Shakespeare's message, with context (for Macbeth: King James, the divine right of kings, belief in witches).

Use short, embedded quotations. Write about Shakespeare as a writer: "Shakespeare presents…", "the audience would…".

CAUTIONContext must explain the play. Do not write a history lesson.

Example exam question

Starting with Macbeth's soliloquy in Act 1 Scene 7, explain how Shakespeare presents Macbeth's ambition.

[34 marks]
  1. Extract: Macbeth admits he has 'no spur' except 'Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself' — the horse-riding image shows ambition that jumps too high and falls
  2. Earlier: after the witches' prophecy his 'black and deep desires' show ambition hidden in darkness
  3. Later: ambition becomes tyranny; he kills Banquo and Macduff's family to keep power
  4. Context: a Jacobean audience believed killing a king broke God's order (divine right of kings); Shakespeare warns that unchecked ambition destroys

Answer: Ambition grows from a private temptation ('Vaulting ambition') into tyranny, and Shakespeare shows it leads to self-destruction.

Modern text essay (e.g. An Inspector Calls)

English Literature · Lit Paper 2 Section A · 34 marks

The picture ideaThere is no extract, so you build the essay from memory. Learn 10 short quotations per theme and the writer's big message.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Literature Paper 2 Section A is worth 34 marks (30 plus 4 for spelling, punctuation and grammar). You choose one of two questions on your modern text. There is no extract.

Plan 4 paragraphs in 45 minutes:

  1. Introduction: the writer's message in one sentence.
  2. The beginning of the text.
  3. The middle: a turning point.
  4. The end, and what the writer wants the audience to learn.

For a play, write about stagecraft: entrances and exits, lighting, stage directions, dramatic irony and the audience.

For example, in An Inspector Calls, Priestley uses the Inspector as his mouthpiece for socialism and collective responsibility. The play is set in 1912 but written in 1945. The audience knows the Titanic sinks and that war is coming, so Mr Birling's confident claims show he cannot be trusted.

CAUTIONWrite about the writer's choices ("Priestley uses…"), not the characters as if they were real people.

Example exam question

How does Priestley use the Inspector to present ideas about responsibility in An Inspector Calls?

[34 marks]
  1. Message: Priestley uses the Inspector to argue that we are all responsible for each other
  2. Beginning: his arrival interrupts Birling's speech about every man looking after himself — a deliberate contrast
  3. Middle: he questions each character in turn; the lighting changes from soft to harsh, exposing the truth
  4. End: his final speech warns of terrible suffering if people do not learn; for a 1945 audience this recalls two world wars
  5. Lesson: the younger characters change, the older ones do not — Priestley puts his hope in the young

Answer: The Inspector is Priestley's mouthpiece: he exposes each character and warns that society must take shared responsibility.

Comparing anthology poems

English Literature · Lit Paper 2 Section B · 30 marks

The picture ideaYou get one poem printed. You bring the second from memory. Pick the partner poem that gives you the most to compare.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Literature Paper 2 Section B is worth 30 marks. One named poem from your anthology cluster is printed. You compare it with another poem of your choice from the same cluster. Spend 45 minutes.

Learn 3 or 4 key quotations for every poem in your cluster, and one context point for each.

Plan 3 or 4 comparative paragraphs. Compare in every paragraph:

  • The poets' ideas and attitudes.
  • Language: imagery, word choice.
  • Form and structure: sonnet, free verse, enjambment, caesura, volta, rhyme.
  • Context: why each poet wrote it.

Example pair (Power and Conflict): Shelley's "Ozymandias" and Blake's "London" both present power. Shelley shows that human power does not last. Blake shows power that oppresses ordinary people.

CAUTIONChoose your second poem before the exam for each likely theme: power, conflict, memory, nature, identity.

Example exam question

Compare how poets present the effects of power in 'Ozymandias' and one other poem from 'Power and conflict'.

[30 marks]
  1. Ideas: Shelley shows a king's power crumbling; Blake shows power still crushing the poor
  2. Language: Shelley's 'sneer of cold command' shows arrogance, whereas Blake's 'mind-forged manacles' suggests people are mentally imprisoned
  3. Form: Shelley's sonnet breaks its rhyme pattern, mirroring the broken statue; Blake's regular quatrains feel like the trapped, repetitive streets
  4. Context: Shelley, a Romantic, attacked tyranny; Blake attacked the church and state in 1790s London

Answer: Both present harmful power; Shelley shows it fades, whereas Blake shows it endures — supported by imagery and form.

19th-century novel essay (e.g. A Christmas Carol)

English Literature · Lit Paper 1 Section B · 30 marks

The picture ideaSame shape as the Shakespeare essay: extract first, then the whole novel, then the writer's purpose in its time.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Literature Paper 1 Section B is worth 30 marks. You get an extract and a question. You write about the extract and the whole novel. Spend 50 minutes.

For A Christmas Carol, the June 2023 question was about the effects of greed. Other likely focuses are Scrooge's change, poverty, family and Christmas.

Plan 4 paragraphs:

  1. The extract: close analysis of two or three quotations.
  2. Earlier or later in the novel.
  3. How the character or theme changes.
  4. The writer's purpose and context (for Dickens: Victorian poverty, the Poor Law, workhouses).

Example: Dickens describes Scrooge as "solitary as an oyster". The simile suggests he is closed and hard on the outside, but there may be a pearl inside — he can change.

CAUTIONKeep quotations short and embedded in your sentence.

Example exam question

Starting with the opening of Stave 1, explain how Dickens presents Scrooge as a cold and isolated character.

[30 marks]
  1. Extract: 'solitary as an oyster' — hard outside, closed off, but with the hint of a pearl inside
  2. Cold imagery: 'The cold within him froze his old features' — pathetic fallacy links his body to winter
  3. Whole novel: by Stave 5 he is 'as light as a feather', showing complete change
  4. Context: Dickens attacks wealthy Victorians who ignored the poor; Scrooge's change shows that anyone can choose generosity

Answer: Cold and oyster imagery present Scrooge as isolated, but Dickens shows he can change, to teach readers social responsibility.

Unseen poetry analysis

English Literature · Lit Paper 2 Section C (27.1) · 24 marks

The picture ideaYou have never seen the poem, so read it three times: once for meaning, once for feeling, once for methods.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Literature Paper 2 Question 27.1 is worth 24 marks. You write about one poem you have not seen before. Spend about 30 minutes.

  1. First read: what is the poem about? Who is speaking?
  2. Second read: how does the speaker feel? Does it change?
  3. Third read: underline 4 or 5 methods: imagery, word choice, rhyme, line length, stanza breaks, the final line.

Write 4 paragraphs, in order through the poem. Always link the method to the poet's idea.

CAUTIONDo not use context. You do not know the poet. Focus on language, form and structure.

Example exam question

Invented poem, 'Lighthouse':
All night it turns its single eye
across the black and heaving bay,
and asks for nothing — not a word —
from ships that pass and slip away.

My mother kept a lamp like this:
a kitchen window, late, aglow.
I never thanked her. Ships don't stop.
The light was there. I didn't know.

In 'Lighthouse', how does the poet present ideas about care?

[24 marks]
  1. Meaning: a lighthouse is compared to the speaker's mother, who waited up for them
  2. Personification 'its single eye' makes the lighthouse a patient watcher that 'asks for nothing'
  3. Structure: the volta at stanza 2 turns from the sea to the mother, revealing the real subject
  4. Short sentences 'I never thanked her. Ships don't stop.' create regret; the final line shows care is often unseen until later

Answer: Through an extended metaphor and a volta, the poet presents care as quiet, constant and unnoticed until it is too late.

Unseen poetry comparison

English Literature · Lit Paper 2 Section C (27.2) · 8 marks

The picture ideaOnly 8 marks, so be quick: two or three sharp comparisons of methods, not ideas.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Literature Paper 2 Question 27.2 is worth 8 marks. You compare the methods the poets use in two unseen poems. Spend about 15 minutes.

This question rewards comparing methods, not meanings. Write 2 or 3 short paragraphs:

  1. Name one similarity or difference in method (imagery, form, structure, tone).
  2. Give a quotation from each poem.
  3. Explain the different effects.

CAUTIONDo not re-analyse the first poem in detail. Compare.

Example exam question

Imagine a second invented poem about a father's packed lunches, written as one stanza that lists objects and ends hopefully. Compare the methods the poets use to present care.

[8 marks]
  1. Both use everyday objects as symbols of care (a lamp; a lunchbox)
  2. 'Lighthouse' uses one extended metaphor, whereas the second poem uses a list, which shows care as many small daily acts
  3. 'Lighthouse' ends with regret ('I didn't know'), whereas the second ends with hope, so the tone is different

Answer: Both use objects; one extended metaphor v a list; regretful v hopeful ending.
