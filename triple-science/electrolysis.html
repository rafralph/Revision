Electrolysis

Explanations 4 ways

1The picture idea

Electrolysis is a tug on the ions. Positive ions go to the negative electrode and gain electrons. Negative ions go to the positive electrode and lose electrons.

2Step by step

An electrolyte is an ionic compound that is molten or dissolved, so the ions can move.

  • At the cathode (negative electrode), positive ions gain electrons. This is reduction.
  • At the anode (positive electrode), negative ions lose electrons. This is oxidation.

For aqueous solutions, water also gives H⁺ and OH⁻ ions:

  • Cathode: the metal forms only if it is less reactive than hydrogen. If not, hydrogen forms.
  • Anode: a halogen forms if halide ions are present. If not, oxygen forms.

Aluminium is extracted from aluminium oxide mixed with molten cryolite, to lower the melting point. The carbon anodes burn away in the oxygen, so they must be replaced.

Half equations (Higher): 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ ; 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.

CAUTIONRemember OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain of electrons.

3Another way to see it

Electrolysis splits an ionic compound using electricity. The ions must be free to move, so the compound is melted or dissolved.

Opposites attract, like magnets: positive ions go to the negative electrode (cathode), and negative ions go to the positive electrode (anode). Memory trick: PANIC = Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.

In solutions, water gives H⁺ and OH⁻ ions too:

  • Cathode: you get hydrogen, unless the metal is less reactive than hydrogen (e.g. copper).
  • Anode: you get oxygen, unless a halide (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) is present, which gives the halogen.

4Watch out for

  • Saying electrons move through the solution. Ions move in the solution; electrons move in the wires.
  • Getting sodium at the cathode from sodium chloride solution. You get hydrogen.
  • Mixing up which electrode is oxidation. Anode = oxidation (loss of electrons); cathode = reduction.
  • Unbalanced half equations, e.g. Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + e⁻. It should be 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.
  • Forgetting why cryolite is used in aluminium extraction (to lower the melting point).

Worked examples 3

Example 1 Warm-up

What is an electrolyte?

    Answer: An ionic compound that is molten or dissolved, so its ions can move

    Example 2 Standard

    Molten lead bromide is electrolysed. Name the products at each electrode.

    1. Pb²⁺ goes to the cathode; Br⁻ to the anode.
    2. Molten, so no water ions.

    Answer: Lead at the cathode; bromine at the anode

    Example 3 Exam-style [3 marks]

    Sodium chloride solution is electrolysed.
    (a) Name the products at each electrode. (b) Write the half equation at the negative electrode.

    1. Cathode (−): sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen gas forms
    2. Anode (+): chloride ions are present, so chlorine gas forms
    3. Half equation: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

    Answer: Hydrogen at −, chlorine at +; 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

    Q1Warm-up

    Why must an ionic compound be molten or dissolved for electrolysis?

    Show answer

    Answer: So the ions are free to move to the electrodes

    Q2Warm-up

    Which electrode do positive ions move to?

    Show answer

    Answer: The cathode (negative electrode)

    Q3Standard

    Name the products when copper sulfate solution is electrolysed with inert electrodes.

    Show answer

    Answer: Copper at the cathode; oxygen at the anode

    Q4Standard

    Write the half equation for chloride ions at the anode.

    Show answer

    Answer: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

    Q5Exam-style

    Explain why aluminium oxide is dissolved in cryolite, and why the anodes must be replaced.

    Show answer

    Answer: Cryolite lowers the melting point, saving energy. Oxygen made at the anode reacts with the carbon anodes to form CO₂, so they wear away

    Q6Exam-style

    Sodium sulfate solution is electrolysed. Name the products and explain them.

    Show answer
    1. Cathode: sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen forms.
    2. Anode: sulfate is not a halide, so oxygen forms.

    Answer: Hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode
