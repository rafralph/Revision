Ecosystems, adaptations and sampling

Biology · rank 2 of 31 · 57 marks · 6.5% of Biology marks · in 4 of 9 papers · Required practical: Field investigation (quadrats)

Explanations 4 ways

1The picture idea

You cannot count every plant in a field. Count a few random samples, find the mean, then scale up to the whole area.

2Step by step

An ecosystem is all the organisms in an area and the non-living parts. AQA tests adaptations, food chains, competition, biotic and abiotic factors, and the quadrat practical.

To estimate a population with quadrats:

  1. Place quadrats at random. Use random numbers for coordinates.
  2. Count the organisms in each quadrat.
  3. Find the mean number per quadrat.
  4. Find the number per m²: mean ÷ area of one quadrat.
  5. Multiply by the total area.

To show how a population changes across a habitat, use a transect line. Put a quadrat at regular intervals along it.

CAUTIONA 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrat has an area of 0.25 m², not 0.5 m².

3Another way to see it

You cannot count every daisy in a football pitch. So you count a few small squares (quadrats) and scale up, like tasting one spoon of soup to judge the whole pot.

  1. Put quadrats down at random (use random numbers for the coordinates).
  2. Count the plants in each one and work out the mean.
  3. Work out how many quadrats would fit in the whole area.
  4. Population = mean per quadrat × number of quadrats that fit.

To see how plants change from one place to another (for example from shade under a tree into a sunny field), put quadrats along a line called a transect.

4Watch out for

  • Placing quadrats where there are lots of plants. This gives bias. Place them at random.
  • Forgetting the quadrat area. A 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrat is 0.25 m², not 0.5 m².
  • Giving the mean per quadrat as the population of the whole field.
  • Mixing up biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) factors.
  • Using a transect when the question asks for a population estimate (random quadrats).

Worked examples 3

Example 1 Warm-up

Is light intensity a biotic or an abiotic factor?

  1. It is not living.

Answer: Abiotic

Example 2 Standard

A field is 50 m × 40 m. Quadrats are 1 m². The mean number of dandelions per quadrat is 4.5. Estimate the number of dandelions in the field.

  1. Field area = 50 × 40 = 2000 m²
  2. 2000 quadrats would fit
  3. 2000 × 4.5

Answer: 9000 dandelions

Example 3 Exam-style [3 marks]

A field measures 20 m by 10 m. A student placed 10 quadrats, each 0.5 m × 0.5 m, at random. The mean number of daisies per quadrat was 3. Estimate the number of daisies in the field.

  1. Area of one quadrat = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m²
  2. Daisies per m² = 3 ÷ 0.25 = 12
  3. Area of field = 20 × 10 = 200 m² ; 12 × 200

Answer: 2400 daisies

Q2Warm-up

Q3Standard

Five quadrats (each 0.25 m²) had 3, 5, 0, 4 and 8 plants. Work out the mean number per m².

Show answer
  1. Mean per quadrat = 20 ÷ 5 = 4
  2. 4 per 0.25 m² → × 4

Answer: 16 plants per m²

Q4Standard

Why must quadrats be placed at random?

Show answer

Answer: So the sample is not biased and represents the whole area

Q5Exam-style

A field is 20 m × 30 m. Ten quadrats of 0.5 m × 0.5 m held 45 buttercups in total. Estimate the number of buttercups in the field.

Show answer
  1. Mean per quadrat = 45 ÷ 10 = 4.5
  2. Quadrat area 0.25 m² → 18 per m²
  3. Field = 600 m² → 600 × 18

Answer: 10 800 buttercups

Q6Exam-style

Describe how a student could find out how the number of daisies changes from under a tree to the middle of a field.

Show answer
  1. Lay a tape measure (transect) from the tree into the field.
  2. Place a quadrat at regular intervals (e.g. every 2 m).
  3. Count the daisies in each quadrat.
  4. Measure light intensity at each point too, and repeat with more transects.

Answer: Use a transect with quadrats at regular intervals; count daisies and measure light
