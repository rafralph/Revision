Negative indices

In-depth topic · 6 parts · 13 diagrams · 23 worked examples · 18 quick-check questions · 21 practice questions

Key factsPart 1Part 2Part 3Part 4Part 5Part 6Exam tipsWatch outPracticeTick off

Key facts

  • a0 = 1 (any number except 0)
  • a−n = 1an: a negative power means one over (the reciprocal)
  • (ab)−n = (ba)n: flip the fraction, then make the power positive
  • Index laws: am × an = am+n, am ÷ an = am−n, (am)n = amn
  • A negative power never makes the answer negative by itself: 2−3 = ⅛, not −8
  • In 3x−2 only x has the power: 3x2. In (3x)−2 both do: 19x2

Part 1 of 6What a negative power means

Start with something you know. Powers of 2 going down the list divide by 2 each time:

24 = 16 → 23 = 8 → 22 = 4 → 21 = 2 → 20 = 1

Nothing stops the pattern at 20. Divide by 2 again and again: 2−1 = ½, 2−2 = ¼, 2−3 = ⅛. A negative power is simply what you get when you keep dividing.

Rule: a−n = 1an. "Minus" in the power means "one over". The size of the power stays the same.

Why a0 = 1

Use the division law: 53 ÷ 53 = 53−3 = 50. But any number divided by itself is 1. So 50 = 1, and the same is true for every base except 0.

Negative base or negative power?

These are different things. (−3)2 = 9 because the base is −3. (−3)−2 = 1(−3)2 = 19. But −3−2 (no bracket) means −(3−2) = −19. Only a negative base, or a minus sign in front, can make the answer negative.

Spot it in the exam

"Write down the value of 4−2", "Work out 10−3" or "Find the value of n". These are usually 1-mark questions. Give an exact fraction, not a rounded decimal.

Part 2 of 6Negative powers of fractions and decimals

"One over" a fraction is the fraction turned upside down: one over 23 is 32. So a negative power on a fraction does two jobs:

  1. Flip the fraction (this uses up the minus sign).
  2. Apply the positive power to the top and to the bottom.

(23)−2 → (32)2 = 94.   (14)−1 → 4.   (13)−2 → 32 = 9.

Decimals

Change the decimal to a fraction first. 0.5 = ½, so 0.5−3 = 23 = 8. 0.2 = 15, so 0.2−2 = 52 = 25. 0.1−3 = 103 = 1000.

Mixed numbers

Change to an improper fraction first. (1¼)−2 = (54)−2 = (45)2 = 1625.

Spot it in the exam

Non-calculator papers love (23)−2 or 0.5−2. A common trap is giving 49: you squared but forgot to flip.

Part 3 of 6Calculating with negative powers

Many questions mix negative powers with ordinary arithmetic. Work each power out first (BIDMAS: indices come before ×, ÷, + and −), then combine the fractions.

2−3 + 4−1 = ⅛ + ¼ = ⅛ + 28 = 38.

3−2 × 62 = 19 × 36 = 4.

Brackets with a negative power outside

Work out the inside first, then flip. (2−1 + 3−1)−1 = (½ + ⅓)−1 = (56)−1 = 65.

Using the same base

Sometimes it is quicker to keep everything as powers. 8−1 × 25 = 2−3 × 25 = 22 = 4.

Spot it in the exam

"Work out the value of …, giving your answer as a fraction" (non-calculator), or "Find the value of n" where 2n equals a combination of powers.

Part 4 of 6Index laws with negative powers in algebra

The index laws work exactly the same with negative powers. The only new danger is the arithmetic with negatives.

  • Multiply → add: x4 × x−7 = x4 + (−7) = x−3.
  • Divide → subtract: x−2 ÷ x−6 = x−2 − (−6) = x4. (Subtracting a negative is adding.)
  • Power of a power → multiply: (x−2)3 = x−6, and (x−2)−3 = x6.

Numbers in front (coefficients)

Deal with the numbers and the letters separately. 6x−2 ÷ 3x−5 = (6 ÷ 3) × x−2 − (−5) = 2x3.

A power outside a bracket applies to everything inside, including the number: (2x−3)2 = 22 × x−6 = 4x−6. And (x−2y3)−2 = x4y−6.

Which part has the power?

3x−2 = 3x2 (only x). (3x)−2 = 19x2 (3 and x). This is a favourite trick question.

Spot it in the exam

"Simplify fully", often with a final answer that must have a positive power, e.g. "Give your answer without negative indices".

Part 5 of 6Writing expressions in the form axⁿ

This turns a fraction with x on the bottom into a power of x. You will use it a lot at A-level, and GCSE questions ask for it directly.

Rule: move xn from the bottom to the top by making the power negative. The number stays where it is.
  • 1x3 = x−3
  • 5x2 = 5x−2 (the 5 was on top already)
  • 14x = ¼x−1 (the 4 was on the bottom, so it becomes ¼)
  • 32x5 = 32x−5 = 1.5x−5

Splitting a fraction

If the top has several terms, divide each one by the bottom: x3 + 2x = x3x + 2x = x2 + 2x−1.

Spot it in the exam

"Write 18x2 in the form axn" or "Express … as a sum of powers of x".

    Part 6 of 6Standard form and solving equations

    Standard form uses negative powers of 10 for small numbers: 0.001 = 10−3, so 0.00032 = 3.2 × 10−4. Multiply in standard form by multiplying the numbers and adding the powers: (4 × 10−3) × (2 × 10−5) = 8 × 10−8.

    Equations with the unknown in the power: make both sides powers of the same base, then the powers must be equal.

    1. Write each side as a power of the same number (2, 3, 5 or 10 usually).
    2. Use the index laws to get one power on each side.
    3. Set the powers equal and solve.

    2x = 116 → 2x = 2−4 → x = −4.

    9x = 127 → 32x = 3−3 → 2x = −3 → x = −1.5.

    2x × 4 = 18 → 2x + 2 = 2−3 → x = −5.

    Spot it in the exam

    "Find the value of x" with powers on both sides, often worth 2–3 marks. Show the "same base" line: it earns a method mark.

    Exam tips

    • Always show the "one over" step: it often earns a method mark even if the arithmetic slips.
    • On non-calculator papers give exact fractions (⅛), not decimals, unless asked.
    • For "find n" questions, change everything to the same base before you do anything else.
    • Check a final answer by estimating: 2−3 must be small and positive.
    • If the question says "without negative indices", move negative powers back to the bottom of a fraction.

    Watch out for

    • Thinking a negative power makes the answer negative: 2−3 is ⅛, not −8.
    • Multiplying the base by the power: 2−3 is not −6.
    • Saying x0 = 0. Anything (except 0) to the power 0 is 1.
    • Applying the power to the number when there is no bracket: 3x−2 = 3/x², not 1/(3x²).
    • Sign errors when subtracting a negative power: x3 ÷ x−2 = x5.
    • Forgetting to flip a fraction: (2/3)−2 = 9/4, not 4/9.
    • Moving the number with the x: 1/(4x) is ¼x−1, not 4x−1.
