Expanding brackets with surds

In-depth topic · 6 parts · 13 diagrams · 24 worked examples · 18 quick-check questions · 21 practice questions

Key factsPart 1Part 2Part 3Part 4Part 5Part 6Exam tipsWatch outPracticeTick off

Key facts

  • √a × √b = √(ab),   √a × √a = a,   √a ÷ √b = √(a/b)
  • Simplify by taking out the biggest square factor: √72 = √36 × √2 = 6√2
  • Only like surds add: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2, but √2 + √3 stays as it is
  • Expand like algebra: every term in one bracket times every term in the other
  • (a + √b)² = a² + 2a√b + b. Never just a² + b
  • (a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b: the surds cancel. This is how you rationalise

Part 1 of 6What surds are and how to multiply them

A surd is a root that does not come out as a whole number or fraction, like √2, √5 or ∛7. As a decimal it goes on forever (√2 = 1.41421…), so we keep it as a surd to stay exact.

Think of √2 like a letter x: it is a fixed number we just do not write out.

Three rules:
√a × √b = √(ab)    √a × √a = a    √a ÷ √b = √(a ÷ b)

√3 × √12 = √36 = 6.   √7 × √7 = 7.   √50 ÷ √2 = √25 = 5.

Numbers in front

Multiply the whole numbers together and the surds together: 2√3 × 5√6 = (2 × 5) × √18 = 10√18. Then simplify (next part): 10 × 3√2 = 30√2.

Simplifying a surd

Find the biggest square number that divides into the number under the root. Split it off and square-root it.

√72 = √(36 × 2) = √36 × √2 = 6√2.   √48 = √(16 × 3) = 4√3.

If you pick a smaller square (√72 = √(4 × 18) = 2√18), you must keep going: 2√18 = 2 × 3√2 = 6√2.

Part 2 of 6Adding and subtracting surds

Treat each surd like a letter. You can only add like surds (the same number under the root), just like 3x + 5x = 8x.

3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2.   7√5 − 2√5 = 5√5.   But √2 + √3 cannot be simplified (like x + y).

Simplify first, then add. Surds that look different may become like surds.

√50 + √8 = 5√2 + 2√2 = 7√2.   √75 − √12 = 5√3 − 2√3 = 3√3.   2√20 + √45 = 4√5 + 3√5 = 7√5.

A common mistake

√9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7, but √25 = 5. So √a + √b is not √(a + b). Adding inside a root is never allowed.

    Part 3 of 6Single brackets

    Multiply the term outside by each term inside, just like 3(x + 2) = 3x + 6.

    √5(3 + √5) = 3√5 + √5 × √5 = 3√5 + 5.

    Method: multiply → use √a × √a = a → simplify any surds → collect like terms → write as a + b√c.

    √2(√6 − √8) = √12 − √16 = 2√3 − 4.

    2√3(√3 − 4) = 2 × 3 − 8√3 = 6 − 8√3 (2√3 × √3 = 2 × 3).

    √6(√2 + √3) = √12 + √18 = 2√3 + 3√2. These are unlike surds, so stop.

    Part 4 of 6Double brackets

    Every term in the first bracket multiplies every term in the second: four multiplications (FOIL: First, Outer, Inner, Last). A grid helps you not miss any.

    (3 + √2)(5 − √2) = 15 − 3√2 + 5√2 − 2 = 13 + 2√2.

    Method: 4 products → whole numbers together → surds together → simplify → a + b√c.

    (2√3 − 1)(√3 + 4) = 6 + 8√3 − √3 − 4 = 2 + 7√3.

    (1 + √5)(3 − 2√5) = 3 − 2√5 + 3√5 − 10 = −7 + √5. (√5 × 2√5 = 2 × 5 = 10.)

    With different surds, some terms may not combine: (√2 + √3)(√2 + √6) = 2 + √12 + √6 + √18 = 2 + 2√3 + √6 + 3√2.

    Part 5 of 6Squaring brackets and the difference of two squares

    Squaring: (2 + √5)2 means (2 + √5)(2 + √5). Write it out twice and do all four products:

    (2 + √5)2 = 4 + 2√5 + 2√5 + 5 = 9 + 4√5.

    Pattern: (a + √b)2 = a2 + 2a√b + b. The middle term 2a√b is the one people forget.

    (√5 − 1)2 = 5 − 2√5 + 1 = 6 − 2√5.   (3√2 − 1)2 = 18 − 6√2 + 1 = 19 − 6√2.

    Difference of two squares: if the brackets are the same except for the sign, the middle terms cancel and the answer has no surd:

    (√7 + 2)(√7 − 2) = 7 − 2√7 + 2√7 − 4 = 3. In general (a + √b)(a − √b) = a2 − b.

    A neat trick: (√3 + √12)2: simplify inside first. √12 = 2√3, so it is (3√3)2 = 9 × 3 = 27.

    Part 6 of 6Rationalising denominators and exam problems

    Rationalising the denominator means getting rid of the surd on the bottom of a fraction. Multiply the top and bottom by the same thing, so the value does not change.

    Bottom is √a

    Multiply top and bottom by √a: 6√3 × √3√3 = 6√33 = 2√3.

    Bottom is a + √b or a − √b

    Multiply by the conjugate (same terms, opposite sign). The difference of two squares removes the surd from the bottom:

    43 − √5 × 3 + √53 + √5 = 4(3 + √5)9 − 5 = 3 + √5

    2 + √32 − √3 × 2 + √32 + √3 = (2 + √3)24 − 3 = 7 + 4√3.

    Exam problems

    • Area of a rectangle with sides (3 + √2) and (3 − √2): 9 − 2 = 7.
    • Pythagoras: square each side (expand), add, then square-root.
    • Find a and b: expand, then match the whole-number parts and the surd parts.

    Exam tips

    • Show every multiplication of the double bracket, even the easy ones. Each line can earn a mark.
    • Always look back at your answer and simplify any surd that still has a square factor.
    • "Give your answer in the form a + b√c" means: whole numbers first, then one surd term.
    • For "show that" questions, every step must be written. Finish by writing the target answer.
    • Check with a calculator if allowed: both sides of your working should give the same decimal.

    Watch out for

    • Writing √a × √a = a². It is just a.
    • Adding inside a root: √2 + √3 is not √5.
    • Squaring a bracket by squaring each term: (2 + √5)² is not 4 + 5.
    • Forgetting to multiply the number in front: 2√3 × √3 = 6, not 3 or 2√9.
    • Leaving √12 unsimplified instead of 2√3.
    • When rationalising, multiplying only the bottom. Multiply top and bottom.
    • Using the same sign instead of the conjugate: for 3 − √5, multiply by 3 + √5.
