Expanding brackets with surds

In-depth topic · 4 parts · 7 diagrams · 10 worked examples · 12 practice questions

Key factsPart 1Part 2Part 3Part 4Watch outPracticeTick off

Key facts

  • √a × √b = √(ab) and √a × √a = a
  • √(ab) = √a × √b: simplify by taking out the biggest square factor, e.g. √50 = 5√2
  • Only like surds add: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2, but √2 + √3 stays as it is
  • Expand surd brackets exactly like algebra: every term times every term
  • (a + √b)(a − √b) = a² − b: the surds cancel (difference of two squares)

Part 1The surd rules you need

A surd is a root that is not a whole number, like √2 or √7. We leave it as a root because it is exact.

  • Multiply: √a × √b = √(ab). So √3 × √12 = √36 = 6.
  • Square: √a × √a = a. So √5 × √5 = 5. This is the most-used rule in this topic.
  • Simplify: find the biggest square factor. √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2.
  • Add: treat √2 like a letter x. 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2 (like 3x + 5x). But √2 + √3 cannot be simplified.

Sometimes you must simplify before adding: √50 + √8 = 5√2 + 2√2 = 7√2.

Part 2Single brackets

Multiply the term outside by each term inside, just like 3(x + 2) = 3x + 6.

√5(3 + √5) = 3√5 + √5 × √5 = 3√5 + 5.

Then simplify any surds: √2(√6 − √8) = √12 − √16 = 2√3 − 4.

Write whole numbers first and surds after if the question asks for the form a + b√c.

Part 3Double brackets and squaring

Multiply every term by every term (four multiplications), then collect like terms. A grid helps.

(3 + √2)(5 − √2) = 15 − 3√2 + 5√2 − 2 = 13 + 2√2.

Squaring: write the bracket twice. (2 + √5)2 = (2 + √5)(2 + √5) = 4 + 2√5 + 2√5 + 5 = 9 + 4√5. It is not 4 + 5: you must include the two middle terms.

Difference of two squares: when the brackets differ only by a sign, the middle terms cancel and the surd disappears. (√7 + 2)(√7 − 2) = 7 − 2√7 + 2√7 − 4 = 3.

Example 7 Exam-style

Expand and simplify (2√3 − 1)(√3 + 4).

  1. 2√3 × √3 = 2 × 3 = 6
  2. 2√3 × 4 = 8√3; −1 × √3 = −√3; −1 × 4 = −4
  3. 6 − 4 + 8√3 − √3

Answer: 2 + 7√3

Part 4Using it: rationalising and exam problems

Rationalising the denominator means removing the surd from the bottom of a fraction. Multiply the top and bottom by the same thing (so the value does not change).

  • Bottom is √a: multiply by √a. 6√3 × √3√3 = 6√33 = 2√3.
  • Bottom is a ± √b: multiply by the conjugate (same terms, opposite sign). The difference of two squares removes the surd:

43 − √5 × 3 + √53 + √5 = 4(3 + √5)9 − 5 = 4(3 + √5)4 = 3 + √5

Other exam problems use the same skills: the area of a rectangle with surd sides, Pythagoras with surd lengths, or "show that" questions where you must reach an exact answer.

Example 10 Exam-style

Show that (√3 + √12)2 = 27.

  1. √12 = 2√3, so √3 + √12 = 3√3
  2. (3√3)² = 9 × 3

Answer: 27 ✓

Watch out for

  • Writing √a × √a = a². It is just a.
  • Adding unlike surds: √2 + √3 is not √5.
  • Squaring a bracket by squaring each term: (2 + √5)² is not 4 + 5. Write it out twice.
  • Forgetting to simplify at the end, e.g. leaving √12 instead of 2√3.
  • When rationalising, multiplying only the bottom. Multiply top and bottom.
  • Using the same sign instead of the conjugate: for 3 − √5, multiply by 3 + √5.

Q12Exam-style

Show answer

  2. Match whole numbers: 3a − 2 = 10 → a = 4
  3. Check the surd part: a − 3 = 1 ✓

Answer: a = 4
