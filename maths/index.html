How each topic is taught

  1. 3Blue1Brown method. Start with a picture. Find the one key idea that makes the rule true. Then show the rule. Each video uses a shape or a moving diagram first and the formula second.
  2. Karpathy method. The explanation is written in about 80% ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English): short sentences, active voice, one instruction for each step, simple words. Each topic also has a diagram and a custom explainer video in the 3Blue1Brown style.
  3. Exam practice. Each worked question is new, but it uses the same style and mark weight as the AQA questions.
1

Probability

147 marks · 7.7% of all marks · in 24 of 24 papers

The picture ideaProbability is area. Draw all outcomes as one square of area 1. Each event cuts the square. The answer is the area of the pieces you need.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Probability is a number from 0 to 1. It shows how likely an event is. A probability of 0 means the event cannot occur. A probability of 1 means the event is certain.

AQA uses tree diagrams, Venn diagrams, relative frequency and "without replacement" questions. To find the probability of two events, one after the other:

  1. Draw a tree diagram. Draw one set of branches for each event.
  2. Write a probability on each branch. Branches from one point must add up to 1.
  3. If the first item is not put back, change the second probabilities. There is one item less in the bag.
  4. Multiply the probabilities along each path.
  5. Add the results of all the paths that you need.

CAUTIONDo not add along a path. Multiply along a path. Add between paths.

Example exam question

A bag has 5 red counters and 3 blue counters. Ali takes a counter at random and does not put it back. He then takes a second counter. Work out the probability that both counters are the same colour.

[4 marks]
  1. P(red, red) = 58 × 47 = 2056
  2. P(blue, blue) = 38 × 27 = 656
  3. P(same colour) = 2056 + 656 = 2656

Answer: 1328

Algebraic manipulation

120 marks · 6.3% of all marks · in 22 of 24 papers

The picture ideaA factorised expression is a rectangle. x² + 5x + 6 is an area with sides (x + 2) and (x + 3). You can cancel a side length. You cannot cancel one piece of the area.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Algebraic manipulation means changing the form of an expression. The value stays the same. AQA tests expanding brackets, factorising, algebraic fractions and changing the subject of a formula.

To simplify an algebraic fraction:

  1. Factorise the numerator (the top).
  2. Factorise the denominator (the bottom).
  3. Find a factor that is in the top and in the bottom.
  4. Divide the top and the bottom by that factor.
  5. Check your answer. Put a number in for x in the start and in the answer. Both values must be the same.

Know these patterns: x² − a² = (x − a)(x + a). For x² + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

CAUTIONCancel only factors. Do not cancel terms that are added or subtracted.

Example exam question

Simplify fully x² − 9x² + 5x + 6

[3 marks]
  1. Top: x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3) (difference of two squares)
  2. Bottom: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) (2 × 3 = 6, 2 + 3 = 5)
  3. (x − 3)(x + 3)(x + 2)(x + 3) → divide top and bottom by (x + 3)
  4. Check with x = 1: 1 − 91 + 5 + 6 = −23 and 1 − 31 + 2 = −23

Answer: x − 3x + 2

Percentages, interest and growth

91 marks · 4.7% of all marks · in 20 of 24 papers

The picture ideaA percentage change is a multiplier. Growth is a chain of the same multiplier. Five years of 3% growth is ×1.03 five times, not 15% once.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A percentage is a number of parts in 100. AQA tests percentage change, reverse percentages, compound interest, depreciation and growth or decay.

To find the value after a repeated percentage change:

  1. Change the percentage to a multiplier. For an increase of r%, use 1 + r ÷ 100. For a decrease of r%, use 1 − r ÷ 100.
  2. Raise the multiplier to the power of the number of years.
  3. Multiply the start value by the result.

To find an original value (reverse percentage), divide the new value by the multiplier.

CAUTIONDo not find the percentage of the new value to go back. Divide by the multiplier.

Example exam question

Sam invests £4000 at 3% compound interest per year. Work out the value of the investment after 5 years.

[3 marks]
  1. Multiplier = 1 + 3 ÷ 100 = 1.03
  2. Value = 4000 × 1.035
  3. = 4637.0963… (simple interest gives only £4600)

Answer: £4637.10

Area, perimeter, arcs and sectors

90 marks · 4.7% of all marks · in 18 of 24 papers

The picture ideaA sector is a fraction of a circle. The angle at the centre gives the fraction. Every sector formula is that fraction of a circle formula.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Area is the space inside a shape. Perimeter is the distance around the edge. AQA often uses circles, sectors, trapeziums and compound shapes.

To find the arc length or area of a sector:

  1. Find the fraction of the circle: angle ÷ 360.
  2. For the arc length, multiply the fraction by the circumference, 2πr.
  3. For the area, multiply the fraction by the circle area, πr².
  4. For the perimeter, add the two radii to the arc length.

Other key areas: triangle ½ × base × height; trapezium ½(a + b)h; any triangle ½ab sin C.

CAUTIONThe perimeter of a sector includes the two straight edges. Many students forget them.

Example exam question

A sector has radius 9 cm and angle 80°. Work out the perimeter of the sector. Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

[4 marks]
  1. Fraction of circle = 80360 = 29
  2. Arc length = 29 × 2 × π × 9 = 4π = 12.566… cm
  3. Perimeter = 4π + 9 + 9 = 30.566… cm

Answer: 30.6 cm

Histograms, box plots and cumulative frequency

89 marks · 4.6% of all marks · in 18 of 24 papers

The picture ideaIn a histogram, area is frequency. A tall bar can hold fewer people than a short wide bar.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

These diagrams show how data is spread. AQA uses all three. They often appear together with a median or interquartile range (IQR) question.

  1. For a histogram, find the frequency density of each class. Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width.
  2. Draw each bar with height equal to the frequency density.
  3. To find a frequency from a histogram, find the area of the bar: width × height.
  4. For a cumulative frequency graph, plot each running total at the upper end of its class.
  5. Read the median at half the total. Read the quartiles at one quarter and three quarters.
  6. For a box plot, mark the lowest value, lower quartile, median, upper quartile and highest value.

CAUTIONThe vertical axis of a histogram is frequency density. It is not frequency.

Example exam question

The table shows the waiting times of 38 people. 0 < t ≤ 10: 8 people. 10 < t ≤ 15: 12 people. 15 < t ≤ 30: 18 people. (a) Draw a histogram. (b) Estimate how many people waited more than 12 minutes.

[5 marks]
  1. (a) Frequency densities: 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8, 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4, 18 ÷ 15 = 1.2
  2. (b) From 12 to 15: width 3 × height 2.4 = 7.2 people
  3. From 15 to 30: all 18 people
  4. 7.2 + 18 = 25.2

Answer: About 25 people

Ratio

82 marks · 4.3% of all marks · in 20 of 24 papers

The picture ideaA ratio is a number of equal boxes. Find the value of one box. Then every other answer is a multiplication.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A ratio compares the sizes of two or more parts. AQA tests sharing in a ratio, ratio with fractions, combined ratios and ratio problems with a difference.

  1. Draw a bar for each person. Draw one box for each part of the ratio.
  2. Find the boxes that match the number you know. This can be one person, the total, or the difference.
  3. Divide the number you know by those boxes. This gives the value of one box.
  4. Multiply to find each share or the total.
  5. Check that the shares match the ratio and the data in the question.

CAUTIONA ratio of 3 : 5 does not mean 3/5. The first person gets 3/8 of the total.

Example exam question

Amy and Ben share some money in the ratio 3 : 5. Ben gets £48 more than Amy. Work out the total amount of money.

[3 marks]
  1. Difference = 5 − 3 = 2 parts
  2. 2 parts = £48, so 1 part = £24
  3. Total = 3 + 5 = 8 parts = 8 × 24
  4. Check: Amy £72, Ben £120, difference £48

Answer: £192

Compound measures

73 marks · 3.8% of all marks · in 19 of 24 papers

The picture ideaThe units are the formula. g/cm³ means grams divided by cubic centimetres. So density = mass ÷ volume.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A compound measure joins two measures. AQA tests speed, density, pressure and rates such as litres per minute. These questions are often worth 3 to 5 marks.

  1. Read the units in the question. The units tell you the formula.
  2. Write the formula. Speed = distance ÷ time. Density = mass ÷ volume. Pressure = force ÷ area.
  3. Change all values to matching units. For example, change minutes to hours.
  4. Rearrange the formula if necessary. Use the triangle: cover the quantity that you want.
  5. Put in the numbers and calculate. Write the units with the answer.

CAUTION20 minutes is not 0.2 hours. It is 20 ÷ 60 = ⅓ hour.

Example exam question

A metal block is a cuboid. It measures 10 cm by 4 cm by 5 cm. The density of the metal is 7.8 g/cm³. Work out the mass of the block.

[3 marks]
  1. Volume = 10 × 4 × 5 = 200 cm³
  2. Mass = density × volume
  3. Mass = 7.8 × 200

Answer: 1560 g

Quadratics

71 marks · 3.7% of all marks · in 16 of 24 papers

The picture ideaCompleting the square is a real square. Split 6x into two strips of 3x around x². One 3 by 3 corner is missing.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A quadratic has an x² term. AQA tests factorising, the quadratic formula, completing the square, quadratic inequalities and turning points.

To solve x² + bx + c = 0 by completing the square:

  1. Halve b. Write (x + b/2)².
  2. Subtract (b/2)². This removes the extra corner.
  3. Add c. Simplify the number.
  4. Make the bracket the subject. Take the square root of both sides.
  5. Write ± in front of the square root. Then solve for x.

The completed square (x + p)² + q gives the turning point (−p, q).

CAUTIONA square root has two values, + and −. Give both solutions.

Example exam question

Solve x² + 6x − 4 = 0 by completing the square. Give your answers in the form a ± √b.

[3 marks]
  1. x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9
  2. x² + 6x − 4 = (x + 3)² − 13 = 0
  3. (x + 3)² = 13, so x + 3 = ±√13
  4. Decimal check: x = 0.606 or x = −6.606

Answer: x = −3 ± √13

Linear equations and inequalities

67 marks · 3.5% of all marks · in 16 of 24 papers

The picture ideaAn equation is a balance. Do the same thing to both sides and it stays level. An inequality is a balance that is already tipped.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A linear equation has no powers of x above 1. AQA tests solving equations, forming equations from words or shapes, and inequalities on number lines.

  1. Expand any brackets.
  2. Move the x terms to one side. Do the same operation to both sides.
  3. Move the numbers to the other side.
  4. Divide both sides by the number in front of x.
  5. For an inequality, show the answer on a number line. An open circle means "not included". A closed circle means "included".

WARNINGIf you multiply or divide an inequality by a negative number, reverse the sign.

Example exam question

Solve 3(2x − 1) < 2x + 9. Then list the integers that satisfy the inequality and x ≥ −1.

[4 marks]
  1. Expand: 6x − 3 < 2x + 9
  2. Subtract 2x: 4x − 3 < 9
  3. Add 3: 4x < 12
  4. Divide by 4: x < 3. With x ≥ −1 the integers are −1, 0, 1, 2

Answer: x < 3; −1, 0, 1, 2

Straight-line graphs

65 marks · 3.4% of all marks · in 19 of 24 papers

The picture ideaTurn a gradient triangle through 90°. 'Along 1, up 2' becomes 'back 2, up 1'. That is why perpendicular gradients are negative reciprocals.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A straight line has the equation y = mx + c. m is the gradient (the slope). c is the y-intercept (where the line crosses the y-axis). AQA tests gradients, parallel and perpendicular lines, and the line through given points.

  1. Find the gradient. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x.
  2. For a parallel line, use the same gradient.
  3. For a perpendicular line, use the negative reciprocal. Turn the fraction upside down and change the sign.
  4. Put the gradient and one point into y = mx + c.
  5. Solve to find c. Write the full equation.

Check: the two perpendicular gradients multiply to −1.

CAUTIONThe gradient is change in y over change in x. Do not put x on top.

Example exam question

Find the equation of the line that passes through (1, 4) and is perpendicular to y = 2x − 5.

[3 marks]
  1. Gradient of y = 2x − 5 is 2
  2. Perpendicular gradient = −½ (because 2 × −½ = −1)
  3. y = −½x + c. Put in (1, 4): 4 = −½ + c, so c = 4.5

Answer: y = −½x + 4.5

Volume, surface area and 3D shapes

64 marks · 3.3% of all marks · in 16 of 24 papers

The picture ideaA prism is a stack of identical slices. Its volume is the slice area times the length. A cylinder is a stack of circles.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Volume is the space inside a 3D shape. Surface area is the total area of all faces. AQA tests prisms, cylinders, cones, spheres, hemispheres and compound solids. The formulas for cones and spheres are given on the paper.

  1. Split the solid into simple parts.
  2. For a prism or cylinder, find the area of the cross-section. Multiply it by the length.
  3. For a sphere, use ⁴⁄₃πr³. A hemisphere is half of this.
  4. For a cone, use ⅓πr²h.
  5. Add the volumes of the parts. Keep π in the working. Round only at the end.

CAUTIONUse the radius, not the diameter. Halve the diameter first.

Example exam question

A solid is a cylinder with a hemisphere on top. The cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. The hemisphere has the same radius. Work out the total volume. Give your answer to 1 decimal place.

[4 marks]
  1. Cylinder: π × 3² × 10 = 90π
  2. Hemisphere: 12 × 43 × π × 3³ = 18π
  3. Total: 90π + 18π = 108π = 339.29… cm³

Answer: 339.3 cm³

Averages and grouped data

62 marks · 3.2% of all marks · in 13 of 24 papers

The picture ideaThe mean is the balance point. Put each value on a number line as a weight. The mean is where a pivot holds the line level.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

An average is one value that represents a set of data. AQA tests the mean, median, mode and range, and estimates from grouped tables. Many questions ask you to compare two groups.

To estimate the mean from a grouped table:

  1. Find the midpoint of each class.
  2. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency.
  3. Add these results.
  4. Divide by the total frequency.

To compare two sets of data, compare an average and compare a spread (range or IQR). Write each comparison in the context of the question.

CAUTIONDivide by the total frequency. Do not divide by the number of classes.

Example exam question

The heights of 20 plants are grouped. 0 < h ≤ 10: 4 plants. 10 < h ≤ 20: 10 plants. 20 < h ≤ 30: 6 plants. Work out an estimate for the mean height.

[3 marks]
  1. Midpoints: 5, 15, 25
  2. Σfx = 4 × 5 + 10 × 15 + 6 × 25 = 20 + 150 + 150 = 320
  3. Mean = 320 ÷ 20

Answer: 16 cm

Indices and surds

61 marks · 3.2% of all marks · in 14 of 24 papers

The picture ideaWalk down the powers of 2. Each step divides by 2. This forces 2⁰ = 1 and 2⁻¹ = ½. The rules are not magic; the pattern makes them.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

An index (power) shows how many times a number multiplies by itself. A surd is a root that is not a whole number, for example √2. AQA tests the index laws, negative and fractional powers, and simplifying surds. Many of these are on the non-calculator paper.

  1. When you multiply powers of the same number, add the indices. When you divide, subtract them.
  2. For a negative index, write one over the positive power: a−n = 1 ÷ an.
  3. For a fractional index, the bottom is the root and the top is the power. Find the root first.
  4. To simplify a surd, find the largest square factor. √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2.
  5. To rationalise a denominator, multiply the top and bottom by the surd.

CAUTIONA negative index does not make the answer negative. 2−2 = ¼.

Example exam question

(a) Work out the value of 8−⅔. (b) Simplify √50 + √18.

[4 marks]
  1. (a) 8−⅔ = 18⅔ ; ∛8 = 2 ; 2² = 4
  2. (b) √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2 ; √18 = √9 × √2 = 3√2
  3. 5√2 + 3√2 = 8√2

Answer: (a) ¼ (b) 8√2

Fractions, decimals and recurring decimals

59 marks · 3.1% of all marks · in 17 of 24 papers

The picture ideaA recurring tail never ends. Shift a copy so the two tails line up exactly. Then subtraction removes the whole infinite tail at once.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Fractions, decimals and percentages are three forms of the same value. AQA tests fraction arithmetic without a calculator, mixed numbers, and changing recurring decimals to fractions (often as a proof).

To change a recurring decimal to a fraction:

  1. Let x equal the decimal.
  2. Count the digits that repeat. Multiply x by 10 for one digit, 100 for two digits, 1000 for three digits.
  3. Write the two equations so the recurring parts line up.
  4. Subtract the smaller equation from the larger one. The recurring parts cancel.
  5. Divide to find x. Simplify the fraction.

CAUTIONIn a "show that" question, write every step. A calculator answer gets no marks.

Example exam question

Prove that 0.4̇5̇ (0.454545…) = 5/11.

[3 marks]
  1. Let x = 0.454545…
  2. Two digits repeat, so 100x = 45.454545…
  3. 100x − x: 99x = 45
  4. x = 4599 = 511

Answer: 5/11 (shown)

Sequences

54 marks · 2.8% of all marks · in 17 of 24 papers

The picture ideaDraw each term as dots. 3, 8, 15, 24 are squares with two extra lines of dots. The n² is a real square you can see.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

A sequence is a list of numbers that follows a rule. The nth term is a formula for any term. AQA tests linear sequences, quadratic sequences, geometric sequences and Fibonacci-type sequences.

To find the nth term of a quadratic sequence:

  1. Find the first differences between terms.
  2. Find the second differences. They must be the same.
  3. Halve the second difference. This is the number in front of n².
  4. Subtract that n² part from each term.
  5. Find the nth term of what is left. It is linear. Add it to the n² part.
  6. Check your formula with n = 1 and n = 2.

CAUTIONThe coefficient of n² is half the second difference, not the full second difference.

Example exam question

Find an expression for the nth term of the sequence 3, 8, 15, 24, 35, …

[3 marks]
  1. First differences: 5, 7, 9, 11. Second difference: 2
  2. ½ × 2 = 1, so the sequence starts with n²
  3. Terms − n²: 3 − 1, 8 − 4, 15 − 9, … = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 → 2n
  4. Check n = 5: 25 + 10 = 35 ✓

Answer: n² + 2n

Sine rule, cosine rule and trig graphs

54 marks · 2.8% of all marks · in 14 of 24 papers

The picture ideaThe cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction. At 90°, cos C = 0 and the correction disappears. Close the angle and the third side shrinks.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

These rules work in any triangle, not only right-angled triangles. Label the sides a, b, c and the opposite angles A, B, C. AQA also tests the graphs of sin, cos and tan and exact values such as sin 30° = ½.

  1. Look at what you know.
  2. If you know two sides and the angle between them, use the cosine rule: c² = a² + b² − 2ab cos C.
  3. If you know three sides and need an angle, rearrange the cosine rule: cos C = (a² + b² − c²) ÷ 2ab.
  4. If you know a side and its opposite angle, use the sine rule: a ÷ sin A = b ÷ sin B.
  5. For the area, use ½ab sin C.

CAUTIONSet your calculator to degrees. Do the 2ab cos C part before you subtract.

Example exam question

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

Circle theorems are rules for angles and lines in circles. AQA asks you to find angles and to give reasons. Some questions ask for a full proof.

  3.   2. Reason: the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference

  2. (b) Left 4: (2 − 4, −3)

A function is a rule that changes an input into an output. AQA tests f(x) notation, composite functions such as fg(x), and inverse functions f⁻¹(x).

  2. For fg(x), do g first. Then put the result into f.

  2. For a regular polygon, each exterior angle = 360° ÷ number of sides.
  3. Interior angle + exterior angle = 180°.
  4. The interior angles of an n-sided polygon add up to (n − 2) × 180°.

  2. Exterior angles add up to 360°
  3. Number of sides = 360 ÷ 24

Two quantities are in proportion when one is a fixed multiple of the other. AQA tests direct proportion (y ∝ x, y ∝ x², y ∝ √x), inverse proportion (y ∝ 1/x, y ∝ 1/x²) and matching graphs to each type.

  2. Put in the pair of values that you know. Solve to find k.
  3. Write the formula with the value of k.
  4. Use the formula to find the missing value.

CAUTIONRead the power carefully. "Proportional to the square of x" means x², not x.

Example exam question

[4 marks]
  1. (a) y = kx²; 36 = k × 9, so k = 4; y = 4x²
  2. (b) y = 4 × 5² = 4 × 25
  3. (c) 64 = 4x² → x² = 16 → x = 4

Similar shapes have the same shape but different sizes. All lengths change by the same scale factor k. Congruent shapes are identical (k = 1). AQA tests similar triangles, area and volume factors, and congruence proofs (SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS).

  2. For areas, use k². For volumes, use k³.
  3. Multiply or divide by the correct factor.
  4. To find k from areas, take the square root. From volumes, take the cube root.

Two cylinders are mathematically similar. Their heights are 6 cm and 9 cm. The smaller cylinder has volume 160 cm³. Work out the volume of the larger cylinder.

[3 marks]

  2. Volume scale factor = 1.5³ = 3.375
  3. Volume = 160 × 3.375

  3. (c) All primes: 2³ × 3² × 5 × 7 = 8 × 9 × 5 × 7

A vector has a size and a direction. AQA tests column vectors, vector paths in shapes, and proofs that points are on a straight line or that lines are parallel.

A scatter graph shows two sets of data for the same items. AQA tests correlation, lines of best fit, outliers, and also pie charts, time-series graphs and sampling.

Explanation (ASD-STE100 style)

An algebraic proof shows that a statement is true for every number, not only for examples. AQA usually asks about odd, even or consecutive numbers.

  1. Write the numbers in algebra. Let n be any integer.
  2. Even number: 2n. Odd number: 2n + 1. Consecutive numbers: n, n + 1, n + 2. Consecutive odd numbers: 2n − 1, 2n + 1.
  3. Write the expression the question describes.
  4. Expand and simplify.
  5. Factorise to show the result. For example, 8n = 8 × n.
  6. Write a final sentence: "8n is a multiple of 8, so the statement is true."

CAUTIONExamples with numbers do not prove anything. You must use n.

Example exam question

Prove that the difference between the squares of any two consecutive odd numbers is a multiple of 8.

[3 marks]
  1. Let the odd numbers be 2n − 1 and 2n + 1
  2. (2n + 1)² − (2n − 1)² = (4n² + 4n + 1) − (4n² − 4n + 1)
  3. = 8n
  4. 8n = 8 × n, and n is an integer, so the result is a multiple of 8

Answer: 8n, a multiple of 8 (proved)

How the data was collected

All question papers came from AQA's own file store. The papers are 8300/1H, 2H and 3H for June 2017, June 2018, June 2019, November 2020, November 2021, June 2022, November 2022 and June 2023. There was no June exam in 2020 or 2021, so the November papers are used for those years. AQA has not released the 2024 and 2025 papers to the public yet, so they are not included. One question in June 2022 Paper 2 (1 mark) was not tagged.

Each question went into one main topic. Questions that test two topics count only for the main topic, so the real share of topics like algebra is a little higher.
