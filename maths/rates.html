Real-life graphs, rates and area under graphs

Rank 19 of 36 · 51 marks · 2.7% of all marks · in 12 of 24 papers

Explanations 4 ways

1The picture idea

Cut the time into thin strips. In each strip, distance = speed × time, which is the strip's area. Add all strips and you get the area under the graph.

2Step by step

A real-life graph shows how one quantity changes with another. AQA tests distance-time graphs, speed-time graphs, conversion graphs and rates of change, often with a tangent or an area estimate.

  1. Read the units on both axes.
  2. The gradient is the rate of change. On a speed-time graph, the gradient is the acceleration.
  3. For a curve, draw a tangent at the point. Find the gradient of the tangent.
  4. On a speed-time graph, the area under the graph is the distance.
  5. Split the area into triangles, rectangles and trapeziums. Add the areas.

CAUTIONOn a distance-time graph the gradient is speed. On a speed-time graph the gradient is acceleration. Check the axes first.

3Another way to see it

Every graph of "something against time" has two secret tools:

  • Gradient = rate of change: how fast the vertical thing changes each second (or hour).
  • Area under the graph = the total: on a speed–time graph, speed × time = distance.

Distance–time graph: gradient = speed, flat = stopped. Speed–time graph: gradient = acceleration, flat = steady speed, area = distance.

Split the area into rectangles, triangles and trapeziums. Find each one and add.

4Watch out for

  • Using distance = speed × time when the speed is changing. Use the area instead.
  • Reading a flat line on a speed–time graph as 'stopped'. It means constant speed.
  • Mixing minutes and hours on the axes.
  • Forgetting that a negative gradient is a deceleration.
  • For average speed, dividing by the number of sections instead of the total time.

Worked examples 3

Example 1 Warm-up

A distance–time graph is a straight line from (0, 0) to (2 hours, 120 km). Work out the speed.

  1. Gradient = 120 ÷ 2

Answer: 60 km/h

Example 2 Standard

A car speeds up from 0 to 12 m/s in 4 seconds. Work out its acceleration.

  1. Gradient = change in speed ÷ time = 12 ÷ 4

Answer: 3 m/s²

Example 3 Exam-style [4 marks]

A car accelerates from rest to 12 m/s in 4 seconds. It travels at 12 m/s for 6 seconds. It then slows down steadily and stops 5 seconds later. (a) Work out the acceleration in the first 4 seconds. (b) Work out the total distance.

  1. (a) Gradient = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s²
  2. (b) Triangle: 12 × 4 × 12 = 24
  3. Rectangle: 6 × 12 = 72
  4. Triangle: 12 × 5 × 12 = 30 ; total 24 + 72 + 30 = 126

Answer: (a) 3 m/s² (b) 126 m

Practice 6 questions · easy to exam-style

Try each one first. Then open the answer to check your working.

Q1Warm-up

A walker covers 300 m in 60 s at a steady speed. What is the gradient of the distance–time graph?

Show answer
  1. 300 ÷ 60

Answer: 5 m/s

Q2Warm-up

What does a horizontal line on a distance–time graph mean?

Show answer
  1. Distance does not change

Answer: The object is stopped

Q3Standard

A speed–time graph is flat at 8 m/s for 20 s. How far does the object travel?

Show answer
  1. Rectangle: 8 × 20

Answer: 160 m

Q4Standard

A bike slows from 15 m/s to rest in 5 s. Work out the acceleration.

Show answer
  1. (0 − 15) ÷ 5

Answer: −3 m/s² (deceleration 3 m/s²)

Q5Exam-style

A train speeds up from 0 to 10 m/s in 5 s, stays at 10 m/s for 15 s, then slows to 0 in 10 s. Work out the total distance and the average speed.

Show answer
  1. Areas: ½ × 5 × 10 = 25, 15 × 10 = 150, ½ × 10 × 10 = 50
  2. Total 225 m in 30 s
  3. 225 ÷ 30

Answer: 225 m; 7.5 m/s

Q6Exam-style

A speed–time curve passes through (0, 0), (2, 6) and (4, 10). Use two trapeziums to estimate the distance in the first 4 s.

Show answer
  1. Trapezium 1: ½ × (0 + 6) × 2 = 6
  2. Trapezium 2: ½ × (6 + 10) × 2 = 16

Answer: 22 m (an estimate)
